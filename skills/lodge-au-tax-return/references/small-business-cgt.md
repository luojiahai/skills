# Small business CGT concessions

Four concessions that reduce, defer or wipe out a capital gain on an **active asset** used in a small business — business premises, goodwill, plant, or shares in an operating company. For a sole trader selling their business or its premises, these are usually worth far more than every deduction in the return combined, so test eligibility before treating the gain as ordinary CGT.

They sit on top of the normal CGT rules in [investments.md](investments.md), not instead of them.

## Step 1 — Basic eligibility

Both gates must open.

**An eligible entity** — one of:
- A **CGT small business entity**: aggregated turnover **under $2 million**.
- The **maximum net asset value test**: the net value of CGT assets owned by the person, their connected entities, their affiliates, and entities connected with those affiliates does not exceed **$6 million**, measured **just before** the CGT event. Not indexed.
- The asset is passively held but used in the business of an affiliate or connected entity.
- A partner in a partnership that is a small business entity, for a partnership asset or a partner's asset used in the partnership's business.

**The active asset test** — the asset was an active asset for:
- at least **half** the test period, where owned for **15 years or less**; or
- at least **7.5 years**, where owned for **more than 15 years**.

**If the asset is a share in a company or an interest in a trust**, three further conditions stack on top:
- The seller either **ran a business just before** the CGT event, or meets the maximum net asset value test.
- Just before the event, the seller was a **CGT concession stakeholder** in that company or trust — a significant individual (at least 20% participation, directly or through interposed entities) or their spouse holding a participation percentage above zero — **or** the CGT concession stakeholders held a total participation of at least **90%** in the entity claiming the concession (the 90% test, which only bites where an entity is interposed).
- The company or trust itself is a small business entity for the year, or meets the maximum net asset value test, applying the modified connected entity rule.
- The shares or interest must satisfy the **modified** active asset test.

Aggregation and control are where this gets genuinely hard — an affiliate's assets, a spouse's company, a family trust can all pull someone over $6 million unexpectedly. Where the total is anywhere near the threshold, price it carefully and apply for a private ruling rather than assuming.

## Step 2 — The order, exactly

The order is prescribed and changes the answer. Follow it literally.

1. **Meet the basic conditions?** No → no small business concessions, but the ordinary CGT discount may still apply.
2. **15-year exemption?** Yes → **disregard the entire capital gain and stop.** No other concession is needed, and nothing further is calculated.
3. **Offset capital losses** against the gain. With more than one gain, the taxpayer chooses which gains the losses reduce.
4. **Apply the CGT discount** if eligible.
5. **Apply the 50% active asset reduction** to what remains.
6. **Apply the retirement exemption and/or the rollover** to what remains.
7. Whatever survives is the net capital gain for the return.

Steps 4 and 5 compound: a $200,000 gain, discounted to $100,000, then halved again, is $50,000 — a quarter of where it started, before the retirement exemption has even been considered.

## The four concessions

**15-year exemption** — the whole gain disregarded. Requires the basic conditions, **continuous ownership for the 15 years** ending just before the CGT event, and the person being **55 or older** with the event happening *in connection with retirement*, or permanently incapacitated at any age. "In connection with retirement" needs a real change: a significant reduction in hours or a significant change in activities. The timing can precede or follow the actual retirement.

**50% active asset reduction** — halves the remaining gain. **Applies automatically** where the basic conditions are met, unless deliberately declined. Decline it only where doing so improves the outcome under the retirement exemption or rollover, and record why.

**Retirement exemption** — disregard up to a **$500,000 lifetime limit per individual**. Not a one-shot: it can be used across several events until the limit is exhausted, so check what the person has used before. Retirement is not actually required — employment, business activities and office holdings can all continue.
- **Under 55**: the exempt amount must be paid into a complying super fund or RSA. To have it count against the CGT cap rather than the non-concessional cap, lodge the **CGT cap election form** with the fund *no later than the time the contribution is made* — late means it eats the non-concessional cap instead.
- **55 or older** just before choosing: no super contribution required.

**Rollover** — defers the gain where a replacement active asset is acquired, or capital improvements made to an existing one, within the **replacement asset period of 2 years** from the CGT event. Miss that window and further CGT events crystallise the deferred gain. The entity choosing the rollover must be the entity that acquires the replacement.

## Making the choice, and proving it

The 50% active asset reduction is automatic. The **15-year exemption, retirement exemption and rollover must each be chosen**, and the choice must be made **before lodging** the return for the year the CGT event happened. Preparing and lodging the return consistently with a concession is generally proof enough of the choice — except the **retirement exemption**, which additionally requires a **written record of the amount chosen to be disregarded**. Write that record into the worksheet and keep it; without it the exemption is exposed.

## What to gather

Purchase contract and date, sale contract and date, everything in the cost base, the years the asset was active in the business, turnover figures for the aggregation test, a net asset valuation just before the event covering affiliates and connected entities, the person's date of birth, and any prior use of the retirement exemption.

A sale year with these concessions in play is worth building the worksheet slowly for, and worth a private ruling on any point that turns on a valuation or on whether an entity is connected.
