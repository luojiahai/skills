# Rental property

One rental schedule per property. Co-owners each report their **legal interest** in every income and expense line — the split follows the title, not who paid the bill, not who earns more, and **not any agreement between the owners, written or oral**. Joint tenants hold equal interests; tenants in common hold whatever shares the title states. Owning and renting one property is not carrying on a business, so it is not a partnership and the shares cannot be varied.

One exception is worth knowing: **interest on money borrowed by one co-owner alone, used exclusively to acquire that person's own interest** in the property, is not divided — it belongs wholly to that owner.

## Income

Rent received, plus: bond retained for damage or unpaid rent, insurance payouts for lost rent, reimbursements from the tenant, and letting or booking fees. Rent is counted when received, not when it fell due. Report **gross** rent — the agent's commission and fees are a deduction, not a netting-off, and agent statements often show only the net remitted.

## The three buckets

Every expense lands in exactly one, and putting a capital item in the immediate bucket is the most common rental error.

**Immediate deduction** — council rates, water, land tax, body corporate fees (administrative fund), insurance, property management fees, advertising for tenants, pest control, cleaning, gardening, **repairs**, and **loan interest**.

**Capital works (Division 43)** — structural work and fixed items, deducted at a low rate over 40 years from completion: a new deck, a retaining wall, re-roofing, a bathroom renovation, new built-in cupboards.

The claim is built on **actual construction costs**, so chase those first rather than buying an estimate. They are more often recoverable than owners expect: the builder's contract and invoices for work the owner commissioned, council development and approval records, strata records for common property, the contract of sale, and the vendor — who may hold the original construction records or a schedule of their own. Where actual costs genuinely cannot be determined, and only then, an estimate from a quantity surveyor or other independent qualified person is accepted, and the fee for that estimate is itself deductible. Deductions can never exceed the actual construction expenditure.

**Depreciating assets (Division 40)** — removable items with their own effective life: oven, dishwasher, air conditioner, carpet, blinds, hot water system. Claimed over that life, or immediately if under the low-cost threshold.

### Repair or improvement

A **repair** restores something to the condition it was in — replacing a few broken tiles, fixing a leaking tap, repainting a worn wall. Immediately deductible.

An **improvement** makes it better than it was or replaces the whole of a thing — re-tiling the bathroom, a new kitchen, replacing the entire fence. Capital.

**Initial repairs** — fixing defects that existed when the property was bought — are capital, no matter when the work happens or how much it looks like a repair.

## Interest

Deductible to the extent the borrowed money was used to produce assessable income. Apportion the loan by **use**, not by the security over it:

- Redrawing for a car or a holiday makes that portion non-deductible, permanently, and mixing a loan this way is very hard to unwind.
- Repayments on a mixed loan are treated as reducing both portions proportionally.
- Only the interest is deductible; principal never is.
- Loan establishment fees and lender's mortgage insurance are borrowing expenses, spread over five years or the loan term, whichever is shorter.

## Apportionment

Deductions run only for the part of the year the property was **genuinely available for rent** — advertised at a market rate, with no unreasonable conditions on who may rent it.

Scale back for: private use by the owner, family or friends; renting below market rate to a relative (deductions capped at the rent received); holiday homes blocked out for personal use; and the part of a property the owner lives in.

For a room or a short-stay listing, apportion by floor area **and** by days available, and remember shared spaces.

## Second-hand depreciating assets

For a residential rental acquired after 9 May 2017, decline in value on **previously used** depreciating assets is generally not deductible — the assets that came with the property. Assets the owner buys new are still claimable, and Division 43 capital works are unaffected. Confirm this against the current ATO page before applying it, and note that the disallowed amounts feed into the CGT cost base instead.

## When the property is sold

The property is a CGT asset — see [investments.md](investments.md). Two rental-specific adjustments: capital works deductions already claimed **reduce the cost base**, which increases the gain, and there is no main residence exemption for the period it was rented unless the **absence rule** covers it. That rule lets a former home keep its main residence status for up to **6 years** while producing income, and **indefinitely** while it is not (left vacant, or used as a holiday house). The 6 years is per absence, not per lifetime: moving back in and later leaving again starts a fresh 6-year period. Beyond 6 years in a single absence, the excess period is taxable. A sale year is the highest-stakes return the owner will file, so work it in this order and show the arithmetic at each step: assemble the full cost base from purchase records, subtract every capital works deduction claimed across all years of ownership, apportion for any period the property was a main residence or not income-producing, then apply the 12-month discount. Reconstructing years of capital works claims from old returns is the slow part; start it before the sale settles rather than in October.
